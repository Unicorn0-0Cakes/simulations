01 What this is

Instruments, not games

There is no score and nothing to win. Each simulation is a working model of a system — a colony, a sealed atmosphere, a shared resource, an oversight regime — with the mechanism written out honestly rather than tuned to feel good.

You get the dials the model actually responds to, and readouts that are allowed to disappoint you.

Everything runs locally

Open a page and it is already running. The whole model lives in the JavaScript your browser downloaded: no backend, no telemetry, nothing recorded. Refresh and the world starts over.

Several were calibrated headlessly against published data before ever being given an interface.

Grounded in real work

Most are built on documented experiments and their real numbers — Calhoun's density study, the Biosphere 2 oxygen anomaly, the long-term E. coli evolution experiment, the public-goods literature.

Where a model departs from the record, the page says so.

02 Why they exist

Some claims about systems are impossible to argue with and impossible to check. Crowding causes collapse. Sorting people by ability makes societies more efficient. Oversight fails because the wrong people are in charge. They sound like conclusions, but they are really compressed stories about mechanisms nobody has laid out.

A simulation forces the mechanism into the open. Once you have to write down how density becomes stress, or how a watchdog gets captured, the claim stops being a slogan and becomes something with parameters — which means it becomes something that can be wrong in a specific way.

What tends to happen next is more interesting than the original argument. Systems built from reasonable local rules produce results nobody chose. Interventions that obviously should work do nothing measurable. A metric refuses to move no matter what you vary, and the reason turns out to be structural rather than a bug.

That last one has its own entry in the catalogue. One simulation here exists solely because two of its parent's outputs were indistinguishable from noise.

The useful moment is not when the model agrees with you. It's when you can say precisely why it doesn't.

Operating principle

03 How to read one

Four habits that make any of these worth more than five minutes of watching dots move.

01

Run it once and don't touch anything

Every simulation opens on a sensible default and starts immediately. Let it play through before you reach for a slider — you need a baseline to have an opinion about.

02

Change one thing

Move a single parameter, reset, and run again. Changing three at once produces a different world but no information about which change caused what.

03

Predict before you look

Say out loud what you expect the readout to do. The value of these things is concentrated almost entirely in the gap between that prediction and the result.

04

Read the notes

Each page documents its own assumptions, its calibration, and what it deliberately does not model. That section is where the honest limits are, and it is short.
