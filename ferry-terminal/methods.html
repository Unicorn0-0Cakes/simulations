01 The source

The research question

A single-berth ferry terminal is a queue that is not served continuously. Between departures nothing happens; at a departure a whole batch leaves at once, or as much of it as fits. The question is how the pattern of arrivals and the discipline used to load the deck jointly determine who waits, for how long, and who does not sail at all.

The model family, and why this one

This is a discrete-event stochastic simulation of a bulk-service queue with deterministic service epochs and finite batch capacity. That structure — service only at discrete epochs, a finite batch, and carry-over of whoever did not fit — is the bulk-service queue of Bailey (1954), written MX/MY/1 in Kendall notation. A continuously available server cannot represent carry-over or denied boarding at all, and denied boarding is not a detail here: Hanssen and colleagues attribute about a fifth of observed terminal waiting to users unable to board their preferred departure because the vessel was full.

The assumption that did not survive

The instrument was first specified with arrivals stationary and independent of the timetable. That assumption was rejected on published evidence before anything was built. Hanssen, Jørgensen and Larsen find that waiting time rises concavely with headway, which they read as fewer users arriving at random as service becomes less frequent; Whitt documents that scheduled and appointment-based systems are frequently under-dispersed relative to Poisson, with variance-to-mean ratios around one half, and warns that lessons from the stationary model do not necessarily carry over.

Rather than replace one fixed assumption with another, arrivals are modelled as an explicit two-component mixture and the mixing fraction ρ is handed to the user as a control. This is a model choice, not a claim that any source specifies ρ. It converts a structural uncertainty into the instrument's central investigable question instead of hiding it inside a parameter.

02 Rules and equations

Arrivals

Vehicle arrivals over the horizon are a two-component mixture.

Random component, fraction ρ of expected volume — a Poisson process, generated by drawing successive exponential inter-arrival times, so that the arrival count is itself a random variable with variance of order λ·T.
Targeted component, fraction (1 − ρ) — for each sailing k a Poisson number of vehicles is drawn, each arriving at tk − D with lead time D drawn from an exponential distribution truncated below the headway.

ρ = 1 recovers pure random arrival; ρ = 0 gives fully timetable-driven arrival. Note what the second component does to the count: it is per sailing and random, not a fixed total distributed over the horizon. Fixing a count and randomising only positions produces an order-statistics process, not a Poisson one, and makes arrival-count variability identically zero. That was a real defect in an earlier build and is now the first thing the model tests check.

Service

At each departure epoch a sailing of capacity C lane-metres departs. The queue is ordered by the active loading policy and boarding proceeds sequentially: a vehicle of length ℓ boards if and only if the running loaded length plus ℓ does not exceed C. This is first-fit, not optimal packing — a terminal loads a queue, it does not solve a bin-packing problem at the ramp. Capacity accumulates in integer centimetres so that floating-point accumulation cannot let a sailing exceed C.

Setting every vehicle length equal reproduces unit-count capacity exactly. The unit-count model is therefore a selectable special case rather than a discarded rival, and which representation to use is a question the user can investigate rather than one the modeller settled.

Carry-over and the two missed-departure measures

Vehicles that do not board remain queued with their accumulated wait and are eligible for the next sailing. Two different things are counted, and they are never added together:

MeasureCountsUnit
Denied boardingA vehicle present at a sailing that did not board itEvents. One vehicle can contribute several.
Never sailedA vehicle still queueing when the horizon endedVehicles. Each contributes at most one.

Right-censored time to service

A vehicle still queueing when observation ends has not produced a missing measurement. It has produced a lower bound: its time to service is at least the time it has already waited. Discarding those vehicles and reporting the distribution of the rest measures only the vehicles a policy chose to serve, which is survivorship bias, and it is severe here because the loading policy is exactly the thing that decides who is left out.

The primary time-to-service display is therefore the Kaplan–Meier product-limit estimator (S6) of the probability that a vehicle is still waiting, computed over observed service times and censored lower bounds together. Censoring marks are drawn on the curve and are never hidden. Where fewer than one in ten vehicles remain at risk, the region is hatched rather than smoothed to look continuous.

The observed-only distribution is retained, because it answers a real question, but it is secondary and there is no view in the instrument that renders it without the served-and-censored composition beside it. There is likewise no interaction path that compares two loading policies on observed-only distributions. That configuration is what made one policy look better than it was, and it is unreachable rather than merely discouraged — see §06.

Stability

load ratio = λ·h ⁄ (C ⁄ 𝔼[ℓ])   — the system is stable when this is below 1.

The ratio is computed against the expected vehicle length, so it is defined under every length distribution and not only when all vehicles are the same size.

03 Parameters

Every default in this instrument is NOT ESTABLISHED. This is the central scientific fact about it. The instrument operates in user-specified parameter space, not in a calibrated empirical space, and the starting configuration is a neutral exploratory point rather than a typical or measured one.

04 What is simplified

  • Loading takes no time. A sailing loads instantaneously at its epoch. Per-vehicle loading time and turnaround dwell are absent because no source gives them, not because they are small.
  • The arrival rate is constant within the run. Non-homogeneous rate profiles λ(t) — the form S5 actually recommends — are not implemented. The mixture captures timetable coupling, not the daily peak.
  • Packing is one-dimensional. Lane-metres only. Real Ro-Ro capacity is constrained by area, weight and floor pressure together (S4).
  • Loading is first-fit within a queue order. No look-ahead, no re-sequencing at the ramp, no partial deck reservation.
  • The loading policies are abstract disciplines. They were not surveyed from practice. Comparing invented policies tests the modeller's imagination unless that is said plainly, so it is said plainly.
  • One berth, one vessel class, no cancellation demand response. A disruption changes a sailing; it does not change how people behave afterwards.
  • No no-shows, no reservations that expire, no pricing.

05 What this cannot establish

The strongest objection, reproduced in full

With every parameter NOT ESTABLISHED, it is a well-structured toy. Its outputs are determined by user-chosen inputs, so any "finding" is a property of the settings, not of ferry terminals. A skeptical operations researcher would say: the batch-service queue is already solved analytically for the simple cases, and the cases the simulation adds — heterogeneous lengths, mixed arrivals — are precisely the ones with no data.

That objection is largely correct and is not dismissed. The defence is narrow and is stated narrowly: the instrument's value is that it makes the structure manipulable. A user can discover that carry-over is a threshold phenomenon, that policy differences may live in the tail rather than in the mean, and that arrival coupling and capacity representation are choices with consequences. Those insights survive parameter ignorance. Any claim beyond them is unsupported.

Equifinality

Different combinations of ρ, λ and C produce similar waiting distributions. The instrument cannot be inverted to infer real parameters from observed waits and must not be presented as if it could.

Specifically not established

  • That any loading policy is operationally better than another. The instrument will not say this and the interface has no path that implies it.
  • Any number for a real terminal, named or unnamed.
  • Any forecast. Nothing here predicts.
  • That the mixed vehicle-length set describes a small terminal. It spans a documented range; it is not a measured distribution.
  • The analytic bulk-service benchmark as a numerical comparison. The primary sources for it were not retrieved, so it has not been checked against the model numerically.

06 Validation

What independent validation found, and what it cost

The first build passed every test written for it and was wrong in four ways. Independent validation found all four. They are recorded here rather than quietly fixed, because how an instrument failed is part of what a reader needs in order to trust it.

DefectWhat was wrongHow it was corrected
D1 — blockingThe random arrival component fixed the arrival count and randomised only positions. Arrival-count variance was identically zero, which no Poisson process has.Rebuilt as an actual Poisson process from exponential inter-arrival times, and the targeted component made per-sailing random too.
D2The load ratio was computed against a single vehicle length, so it was unavailable under the mixed distribution — the very case where capacity representation matters.Computed against expected vehicle length, defined under every distribution.
D3Sailings were processed by index. A delay larger than one headway let a sailing be processed before one that departed earlier.Fixed at the scheduling layer — the event loop is driven by actual departure time — not by sorting the output afterwards. Sailing identity is preserved.
D4Time to service was reported over served vehicles only. Under a policy that leaves long vehicles behind, that measures the survivors of the policy's own selection.Resolved by changing what is measured, not how it is displayed: censored lower bounds are recovered for every unserved vehicle and the primary estimate is Kaplan–Meier.

What correcting D4 revealed

Before the correction, the shortest-first policy showed a shorter observed median time to service than first-come — 278 minutes against 391 — and moved about one per cent more lane-metres. After the correction, in the same configuration, it served zero long vehicles against first-come's 4.7 per run: a long-vehicle service rate of 0.0000 against 0.0913. Those figures are from the validation run of the Python reference implementation, which is where the defect was found. The flattering conclusion did not survive the better measure. Correcting the measure did not make the conclusion available; it made the bias visible. This is why the instrument has no view that compares two policies on served vehicles alone.

The instrument reproduces the signature rather than the numbers, as it must, because it uses a different generator: in the regression suite on this page's model, first-come serves 16.1 per cent of long vehicles and shortest-first serves 0.0 per cent, on one shared arrival realisation.

The stability test, including the version that was wrong

Validation first asserted that no vehicle should be left unserved below the stability boundary, and reported three mismatches against that expectation. The expectation was the error, not the model: a finite horizon leaves vehicles queueing near the boundary regardless of stability. The corrected criterion is the signature — a small, roughly flat count below the boundary and an order-of-magnitude climb above it — and it is what the test suite checks. The mistaken expectation is preserved in the validation record rather than deleted.
